# Appendix A: Complete Survey Instrument

## A.1 Pre-Session Survey

Administered before the session to capture baseline context:

- Primary role in computing education (select all that apply)
- Area(s) of computing education the participant's own documents relate to
- Primary context for the session (personal/private, classroom/instructional,
  community/group, professional)
- Frequency of working with the computing-education documents used in the
  session
- Types of computing-education documents used
- Typical weekly use of these documents (up to 3 selected)
- Step-by-step description of how the participant normally finds/verifies
  information in their documents
- Frequency of AI tool use (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, etc.) for
  computing-education work
- What AI tools are used for, if applicable
- Comfort uploading computing-education documents to a cloud-based AI tool
- If hesitant, what drives the hesitation
- Open response: what would make a document-grounded assistant "worth
  using" in the participant's computing-education context

## A.2 Exit Survey — 30-Item Likert Scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree)

Six constructs, mapped to items as analyzed in the paper (Table with
construct-level statistics):

**Accuracy & Faithfulness** (items 1–6, $\alpha = .84$)

1. The assistant's responses were accurate given my documents.
2. The assistant correctly attributed information to the right source or section.
3. The assistant preserved important nuances and qualifications from my documents.
4. When the assistant included code, the code was syntactically correct.
5. When the assistant explained computing concepts, the explanations were accurate.
6. The assistant avoided generating information not present in my documents.

**Trust & Reliability** (items 7–11, $\alpha = .79$)

7. I felt I could trust the assistant's outputs.
8. The assistant's tone of confidence matched the quality of its answers.
9. I could tell when the assistant was uncertain or guessing.
10. I would trust the assistant enough to use its output without additional verification.
11. The assistant's handling of ambiguous or edge-case questions was appropriate.

**Perceived Usefulness** (items 12–17, $\alpha = .67$)

12. The assistant would be useful in my real computing-education work.
13. The assistant saved me time compared to my usual approach.
14. The assistant helped me think about my documents in new ways.
15. The assistant would be useful for preparing computing-education materials.
16. The assistant would be useful for answering student questions about my documents.
17. I would recommend this tool to a colleague in computing education.

**Response Quality** (items 18–22, $\alpha = .49$)

18. The assistant's responses were clear and well-organized.
19. The assistant used appropriate technical vocabulary for computing topics.
20. The assistant's level of detail was appropriate for my needs.
21. How the assistant showed where it found information was helpful.
22. The amount of context the assistant used in responses was appropriate.

**Pedagogical Suitability** (items 23–26, $\alpha = .52$)

23. The assistant's explanations would be appropriate for my students' level.
24. The assistant respected pedagogical intent (e.g., didn't give away answers meant for discovery).
25. The assistant could support scaffolding or guided inquiry in computing.
26. I would trust the assistant to interact directly with students using my materials.

**Privacy & Data Comfort** (items 27–30, $\alpha = .47$)

27. I felt comfortable uploading my computing-education documents to this system.
28. Knowing data stays on my device (local-first) increases my willingness to use it.
29. This tool would be compatible with my institution's policies on AI use.
30. I would feel comfortable using this tool with materials containing student information (if de-identified).

## A.3 Post-Session Open-Ended Prompts

- Narrative summary: what happened during the session, what the participant
  tried/asked/noticed (4–8 sentences)
- What felt most natural, and least natural, about using the assistant
- Where this would fit (if anywhere) in the participant's real workflow
- What the participant relied on to judge whether an answer was trustworthy
- Accuracy breakdown by content type (code, conceptual explanations,
  pedagogical content, citations/attribution) — tested vs. not tested, and
  outcome
- Whether the assistant produced any output that would be harmful if shared
  with learners
- One moment the tool helped, and one moment it failed or felt risky
- Workarounds used (checklist + free text)
- What the participant would need to be accountable for using these outputs
  in a real setting (with students, colleagues, a department chair)
- Whether the participant would be comfortable sharing the assistant's
  output directly with students, and why or why not
- What categories of materials the participant would never upload, and why
- How local-first storage would change willingness to use the tool, what
  concerns it addresses, and what would remain
- Whether there are academic-integrity implications of using a tool like
  this in the participant's context
